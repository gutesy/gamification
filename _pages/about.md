Meta-Analysis Summary
-

The Gamification of Learning: a Meta-analysis

Michael Sailer & Lisa Homner

Published online: 15 August 2019 

https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w 

Focus of the study
-
The primary focus of this study is a systematic meta-analysis designed to synthesize research findings on the effectiveness of gamification in the context of learning and education. The researchers aimed to evaluate how gamification (In recent years, the concept of gamification, defined as “the use of game design elements in non-game contexts” (Deterding et al. 2011, p. 9)), affects cognitive, motivational, and behavioral learning outcomes compared to conventional instructional methods. Beyond assessing general effectiveness, the study explores the "how" of gamification by investigating several moderating factors that might contribute to its success.

Introduction
-
Gamification, which involves the use of game design elements in non-game contexts, serves as a strategic design process to augment or alter existing learning activities to make them more active and engaging. According to the Theory of Gamified Learning, gamification does not replace traditional instruction but improves its effectiveness by targeting specific behaviors and attitudes that mediate the relationship between instructional content and final learning outcomes, though high-quality content remains a necessary prerequisite for success. This approach is psychologically grounded in Self-Determination Theory, which suggests that learning is most effective when the environment satisfies the human needs for competence, autonomy, and social relatedness. Game elements are specifically designed to trigger these needs; for example, game fiction—including narrative stories and avatars—can satisfy autonomy and relatedness, while consistent feedback fosters feelings of competence. Furthermore, social interactions such as constructive competition, which blends competitive pressure with collaborative support, can significantly enhance intrinsic motivation by fostering social relatedness. While empirical interest in this field has surged, previous systematic reviews have often produced mixed or inconclusive results, primarily because much of the existing research has been criticized for a lack of methodological rigor and theoretical foundation. Prior to this work, meta-analytic data was limited to small, underpowered samples focusing mostly on motivation, creating a clear need for a comprehensive synthesis that investigates cognitive, motivational, and behavioral outcomes alongside various conceptual and situational moderators to determine not just if, but how gamification facilitates learning.

Purpose of the Current Study
-
Building upon the "positive tendency" but inconclusive evidence found in prior systematic reviews and methodologically limited meta-analyses, this study aims to provide a definitive statistical synthesis of gamification’s effectiveness in educational contexts. The primary purpose is to provide conclusive evidence on how gamification affects three distinct domains of learning: cognitive learning outcomes, which encompass both conceptual knowledge of facts and principles as well as application-oriented procedural and strategic knowledge; motivational learning outcomes, which adopt a broad view including intrinsic motivation, attitudes, engagement, and self-efficacy; and behavioral learning outcomes, which focus on the development of technical skills, motor skills, and task-specific competences. A central goal of this research is to move beyond the binary question of whether gamification works to investigate how and under what conditions it succeeds by analyzing various conceptual, situational, and methodological moderators. To ensure the stability and robustness of the findings, the authors also utilize subsplit analyses to isolate studies characterized by high methodological rigor and evaluate potential publication bias. Finally, the study highlights a critical distinction in the current literature regarding assessment timing: while cognitive and motivational outcomes are almost exclusively measured after the intervention, behavioral outcomes are typically captured as process data during the intervention itself, potentially influencing how these effects are interpreted.

Moderating Factors
-
Because gamification can be implemented through a wide variety of design elements that result in diverse affordances, social interactions, and learning arrangements, the researchers included moderating factors to account for this significant conceptual heterogeneity. The inclusion of these factors was also necessary to address the impact of situational and contextual variables and to account for the widespread lack of methodological rigor observed in previous primary studies. The selection of these specific moderators was an iterative process, focusing on variables that were both theoretically interesting and supported by a large enough body of literature to allow for meaningful statistical synthesis. Ultimately, the study examined conceptual moderators (game fiction and social interaction), instructional moderators (learning arrangement of the comparison group), situational/contextual moderators (intervention duration and research context), and methodological moderators (randomization, study design, and the type of measurement instruments used).

Method
-
The researchers conducted a broad literature search using the terms "gamification" and "gamif*" across multiple academic databases and Google Scholar, without restricting publication years. They avoided using specific game design elements as search terms to prevent bias, since gamification research lacks consistent terminology. After removing duplicates, 5548 records were identified.
Only studies where gamification (the use of game design elements in non-game contexts) was clearly used as an independent variable were included. Studies using the term "gamification" but not matching this definition were excluded. Learning Outcomes: Studies had to measure at least one learning outcome, categorized as: Cognitive, Motivational and Behavioral. Only studies published in English were considered. Only primary, quantitative studies with human participants were eligible. Studies had to use a between-subjects design, comparing at least one gamification condition to another instructional approach. Descriptive studies and those without group comparisons were excluded. Studies had to compare gamification to a non-gamified instructional method, not to fully developed games. Studies needed to report enough statistical information to allow for meta-analytic calculations.

The coding process began with a systematic screening of titles to exclude ineligible publications, followed by abstract coding and a final full-text review of remaining eligible articles. To ensure consistency and reliability, two independent coders evaluated moderator variables on a random sample of approximately twenty-five percent of the studies, achieving high interrater reliability with kappa values ranging from 0.76 to perfect agreement. Any discrepancies identified during this initial phase were resolved through mutual discussion before the rest of the studies were coded, and all statistical data were double-coded and recalculated when necessary. The moderator for game fiction was determined by the presence or absence of a narrative context or game world, such as stories or avatars. Social interaction was categorized based on whether learners competed, collaborated, engaged in both competitive and collaborative activities, or worked entirely alone. The learning arrangement of the comparison groups was coded as passive instruction, active instruction, mixed instruction, or untreated waitlist conditions. Intervention duration was categorized into periods ranging from one day or less to more than half a year, while the research context was identified as school, higher education, work-related, or informal training settings. Methodological moderators included randomization, which distinguished between experimental and quasi-experimental designs, and study design, which noted whether measures were posttest-only or pre- and posttest. Finally, the types of measurement instruments used were classified as standardized, adapted, or self-developed. These rigorous procedures were designed to identify various factors that could potentially influence the effectiveness of gamification on learning outcomes.

Final Sample
-
The application of the study’s exclusion criteria initially resulted in a final sample of 38 publications reporting 40 experiments, though further refinements were made to ensure statistical integrity. To avoid bias in the results, three studies by Wasson et al. (2013), Grivokostopoulou et al. (2016), and Sanmugam et al. (2016) were excluded from the cognitive learning analysis because their recorded effect sizes were found to be extraordinarily large outliers in the forest plots, whereas no such outliers were identified for motivational or behavioral outcomes. Additionally, reports by de-Marcos et al. (2014), Su and Cheng (2014), and a portion of the work by Hew et al. (2016) were removed because they contained redundant experiments already accounted for in more extensive studies within the sample. Following these adjustments, the final sample for cognitive learning outcomes consisted of 19 independent experiments published between 2013 and 2017 involving a total of 1,686 participants. The analysis for motivational learning outcomes included 16 independent experiments published in the same period with 2,246 participants, while the behavioral learning category consisted of 10 independent experiments from nine primary studies published between 2014 and 2017, examining a total of 951 participants.

Results
-
The results of this meta-analysis indicate that gamification generally has significant, small positive effects on learning across cognitive, motivational, and behavioral domains. Cognitive learning outcomes showed the largest effect size at g = 0.49, followed by motivational outcomes at g = 0.36 and behavioral outcomes at g = 0.25. While these effects were statistically significant, subsplit analyses including only high-rigor studies demonstrated that only the cognitive outcome remained stable and significant. In contrast, summary effects for motivational and behavioral outcomes became non-significant when restricted to experimental and high-quality quasi-experimental designs, indicating that success in these areas depends heavily on specific design factors. One such critical factor was the mode of social interaction, as combining competition with collaboration was found to be significantly more effective than solitary engagement or mere competition for both motivational and behavioral performance. Specifically, the subsplit analysis revealed that constructive competition outperformed pure competition for these outcomes. Furthermore, the inclusion of game fiction, such as narrative stories and avatars, significantly moderated behavioral outcomes but did not show a significant impact on cognitive or motivational results. Regarding the research context, gamification was found to be significantly more effective for cognitive learning in school settings compared to higher education or informal training environments. The duration of the intervention also played a role in motivation, with interventions lasting between one month and half a year producing significantly larger effects than those lasting one day or less. Methodological rigor also influenced reported outcomes, as quasi-experimental studies often yielded significantly larger effect sizes for motivational and behavioral learning than randomized experimental studies. Finally, assessment timing emerged as a key distinction, as behavioral data were almost exclusively collected as process data during the intervention, while cognitive and motivational outcomes were typically assessed afterward.

Limitations
-
This meta-analysis faces four main limitations. First, small sample sizes, particularly for behavioral learning outcomes and subgroup analyses, which limits statistical power and generalizability. Second, a lack of methodological rigor in many primary studies, which may have led to overestimated effects for motivational and behavioral outcomes. Third, a lack of standardized measurement instruments, making it difficult to compare results across different studies. Fourth, the timing of behavioral outcome measurements, which were collected during interventions rather than after, leaving open the question of whether gamification truly improves long-term skills or just short-term performance during the game.
